Pick up a jar of old marbles and you will find dozens of different looks. Collectors have names for all of them, and the names can be confusing, because the same word means different things in different places. This guide covers the main types a beginner is likely to meet, grouped by what they are made of and how they were made.

## Clay and china

### Clay marbles

Clay marbles are the plain, slightly rough ones, often tan, sometimes dyed red, blue, green or yellow. The Marble Collectors Society of America calls them "the most common old marble that you will find" [2]. Wikipedia notes they were made in huge numbers in the 1800s and early 1900s, which is why they are sometimes called **commies**, short for "commons" [1].

A glazed version, usually brown or blue with small light "eyes" where marbles touched in the kiln, is known to collectors as a **Bennington**. The name is a misnomer: it appears that all of these marbles were imported from Germany [2].

### China marbles

**Chinas** are made of glazed porcelain, often with painted lines or patterns [1].

## Stone and steel

### Aggies

An **aggie** is a marble made of agate, or of glass made to look like agate [1]. Real stone marbles were ground into spheres, and German glassmakers took inspiration from the polished agate spheres of the gemstone town of Idar-Oberstein [10]. Britannica lists onyx and agate among the materials marbles have been made from [9]. Because people use "aggie" for both stone and glass, ask which one is meant.

### Steelies

A **steelie** is a steel marble. Old ones were made from folded sheet steel, and later ones are solid ball bearings [1]. Steelies were often used as shooters [13], but tournaments ban them: in the US National Marbles Tournament, a shooter must be glass or stone, never metal [11].

## Handmade German glass

These are the marbles collectors prize most. Most were made in Germany between about 1850 and the Second World War, and every handmade marble has at least one small rough **pontil** mark where it was cut free [3].

### Swirls

A **swirl** has bands or threads of color twisting from pole to pole. Handmade swirls get their twist from the way they were turned while being made [6]. Machine-made swirls exist too, and Wikipedia describes the common "swirly" as a glass marble with one swirly color [1].

### Onionskins

An **onionskin** is a handmade swirl whose surface is covered with many closely packed streaks of color, a bit like the layers of an onion [1].

### Lutzes

A **Lutz** has bands of glittering metallic flecks in the glass, made with finely ground copper flakes or goldstone. The bands are usually edged with thin white strands [4]. There are banded, onionskin, ribbon and other Lutz varieties [4]. The name is often linked to the glassmaker Nicholas Lutz, but he is wrongly credited with them [1].

### Sulphides

A **sulphide** is a large clear glass marble with a small white figure inside, usually an animal, sometimes a person or a religious figure. The figure is porcelain, not sulfur. Early collectors simply assumed it was sulfur, and the name stuck. Sulphides were made in Lauscha, Germany, from the mid-1800s until 1936 [5].

## Machine-made glass

From the early 1900s, machines took over toy marble making [3].

### Clearies

A **clearie** is a plain transparent marble, clear or colored, with nothing inside [1][6]. It is sometimes called a crystal or a purie [1].

### Cat's eyes

A **cat's eye** is a clear marble with colored vanes inside, like the pupil of a cat's eye [1][7]. By the 1950s they were common. The Marble Collectors Society of America says they became the main marble after the Second World War, made in East Asia and in the US [6]. Marble King's Roger Howdyshell is credited with the first American-made cat's eyes [7], but who made the very first one is disputed.

### Other names you will see

Machine-made marbles come in many patterns, and makers gave them their own names. A few examples from Akro Agate of West Virginia, which made marbles from 1910 to 1951 [8]:

- **Corkscrews**: two or more spirals of color winding from pole to pole.
- **Oxbloods**: a deep red patch or swirl with fine black threads in it.
- **Moonies**: milky white opalescent glass.

## Shooters and taws

The **shooter**, or **taw**, is the marble you shoot with, usually a little bigger than the others [1]. In Britain it is often called a **tolley** [12]. Kids have plenty of other names for it. A survey by the Dictionary of American Regional English collected "shooter", "steelie", "boulder" and "aggie", among others [13].

Tournaments set limits:

- **US National Marbles Tournament:** shooter between 1/2 and 3/4 inch across, glass or stone only [11].
- **British and World Marbles Championship:** a tolley up to 3/4 inch across, glass or ceramic [12].

## Sizes

Marbles come in many sizes. Wikipedia gives a typical range of about 0.5 to 3.5 cm (0.2 to 1.4 inches) across [1].

- **Standard play marbles** are about 5/8 inch, or 16 mm. The US tournament uses 5/8 inch targets [11], and the Spielwarenmesse magazine gives 16 mm as the size for club play [10].
- **Shooters** are usually a bit bigger, up to about 3/4 inch for tournaments [11][12].
- **Big marbles**, like many modern art marbles, can be around 2 inches (5 cm) across. Wikipedia notes these are sometimes called "toe breakers" [1].

## A note for new collectors

Marbles have been reproduced since at least the late 1970s [6], and some types are easy to confuse. If you think you have something old or rare, compare it with a collectors' society guide, or ask the Marble Collectors Society of America. We do not value or identify marbles.
